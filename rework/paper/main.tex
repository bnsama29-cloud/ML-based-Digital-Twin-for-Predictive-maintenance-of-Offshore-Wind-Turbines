\begin{abstract}
Autoencoders trained on normal operation are widely used to flag faults in wind turbine SCADA data, but their reconstruction error also follows wind and load. This paper tests a common remedy, scoring only the error that operating conditions do not explain, together with causal smoothing, on all 95 datasets of the CARE-to-Compare benchmark (three farms, 45 faults, 50 normal periods). Splits are ordered in time, all settings are fixed before testing, and six detectors are compared (three seeds for the stochastic ones) with the benchmark's CARE score and a paired bootstrap over datasets. The dense autoencoder scores 0.584 and 0.592 with normalisation and smoothing, against 0.5 for random predictions; PCA and a conditional autoencoder are statistically level with it, an LSTM autoencoder is lower (0.531) and a sigmoid output costs 0.086. Normalisation cuts false alarms but hides load-dependent fault signal, and helps weak detectors most (Isolation Forest +0.047). Thresholds tuned on two farms and tested on the third lift each detector's full pipeline to 0.58--0.61, below the 0.66 of a per-farm tuned autoencoder. Plant-area attribution names the operator's root cause first in 37\% of 40 faults, against 12\% for random and 22\% for raw z-scores, and a causal health index gives a work-order trigger with a time-to-threshold band on a generator-bearing fault.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Operation and maintenance make up a large share of the cost of wind energy, above all offshore \cite{r20}. Industry is moving from reactive and preventive maintenance to predictive maintenance, which acts on the condition of the machine to avoid unplanned downtime \cite{r5,r8}. A middle path is to read the SCADA archive, mark unusual behaviour early, and only then place a work order \cite{r20}.

Normal behaviour models do this by learning what the sensors look like when the turbine is healthy and flagging departures from it \cite{r21,r22,r23}. Autoencoders are popular because one network can watch hundreds of channels at once \cite{r10,r11,r25}. Their reconstruction error, however, depends on the operating point: a healthy turbine at rated power and one idling in light wind do not have the same typical error. The benchmark used here handles this with an adaptive threshold, a second network that predicts the expected error, tuned per farm \cite{r12}, and the same group has built a labelled fault-detection benchmark from service data of district-heating substations \cite{r19}. Results are hard to compare, however, when they come from one turbine, one fault, or a random split that places fault rows in the training set \cite{r12,r28}.

This leaves a gap: no study we know of tests these corrections on every fault and normal period of a public benchmark with all settings fixed in advance, separates the effect of the method from the effect of tuning, and checks the resulting fault attribution against what operators actually repaired. The contributions of this paper are:
\begin{itemize}
\item a leakage-safe evaluation of condition normalisation and causal smoothing on all 95 CARE-to-Compare datasets, across six detectors, three seeds and an ablation of every step, with a paired bootstrap over datasets;
\item leave-one-farm-out threshold tuning, which shows how much of the gap to a tuned benchmark result comes from tuning rather than from the detector;
\item a check of plant-area attribution against operator fault notes, against random, majority-class and model-free floors;
\item a causal health index and time-to-threshold estimate with a 95\% band, a work-order gate sensitivity study, and compute times of the main detectors.
\end{itemize}
Code, settings and all result tables are public.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/bnsama29-cloud/ML-based-Digital-Twin-for-Predictive-maintenance-of-Offshore-Wind-Turbines}}

\section{Related Work}
SCADA-based condition monitoring is reviewed in \cite{r20}. Early normal behaviour models predicted one temperature from a few operating inputs and flagged large residuals \cite{r21,r22,r23}; change-point detection keeps such models robust to shifts caused by malfunctions and maintenance actions \cite{r24}. Inputs physically coupled to the target improve the fit but can hide the fault \cite{r26}, a point that returns in our results. Deep networks such as LSTM autoencoders have since been applied to wind turbine data \cite{r2,r25}, and autoencoders treat a large reconstruction error as a sign that the current row does not look like healthy training rows \cite{r10,r11,r29}. Recent work at this conference applied autoencoders to microgrid faults \cite{r31}, anomaly flags to wind power prediction \cite{r30}, deep learning to appliance maintenance \cite{r32} and noise tests to line-fault classifiers \cite{r33}; we extend this line to a multi-farm benchmark with time-ordered splits.

The work closest to ours comes from the group that released CARE-to-Compare. ARCANA attributes an autoencoder anomaly to input features through a sparse correction of the input \cite{r17}, and transfer learning lets a model trained on one turbine serve another \cite{r18}. The benchmark paper defines the CARE score and reports 0.66 for an autoencoder whose layer sizes, learning rate and thresholds were tuned per farm, with an error-predicting adaptive threshold on farms A and B, and 0.5 for random predictions \cite{r12}. Our condition normalisation is a post-hoc relative of that adaptive threshold; the difference is that nothing is tuned on test data. On the same data, a VAE, LSTM-autoencoder and Transformer ensemble reports a row-level AUC-ROC of 0.947 \cite{r29}, but with a 70/15/15 split and without the benchmark prediction windows or CARE score, so its numbers cannot be compared with \cite{r12} or with ours. Digital-twin surveys \cite{r7,r8} and structural twins \cite{r14,r15} motivate the playback view; reporting follows the REFORMS checklist \cite{r28}. \section{Dataset and Leakage Rules}
CARE-to-Compare \cite{r12} holds 95 datasets from 36 turbines in three farms (Table~\ref{tab:data}).\footnote{Counts are taken from the event files of the Zenodo version used (record 15846963): 45 fault and 50 normal datasets; the dataset paper \cite{r12} lists 44 and 51.} Farm A is onshore in Portugal (86 columns); farms B and C are offshore in Germany (257 and 957 columns). Each dataset covers one turbine with about a year of training data and a prediction window of 4 to 98 days that holds either a labelled event ending in a fault or a normal period. Status codes 0 and 2 count as normal operation; farm A's codes come from the fault logbook, so they choose training rows but are ignored in evaluation, as in the benchmark's reference code. Each farm publishes a plain description of every sensor, and most faults carry a short operator note on the root cause.

All rules were fixed before any test result was seen. Leakage, information from the test period reaching the model, is the main reason SCADA results fail to transfer: a random split puts rows from days before a fault into training, so the model learns the fault as normal and the test rows next to them become easy to predict \cite{r28}. Here every statistic is fitted on the training year only and the prediction window is never seen before scoring. Models use only normal-status training rows. Following the filter of \cite{r12}, rows where the wind is in the operating range but power is near zero are also dropped (here: wind 5--20~m/s and power below 2\% of its training 99th percentile), since a turbine standing still in good wind is curtailed or stopped, not healthy. The last 15\% of the remaining rows are held out and split in time into two calibration slices, $\mathcal{C}_a$ for the condition model and $\mathcal{C}_b$ for the threshold, so neither is fitted on rows used to set the other. Identifiers and the status code are removed from the inputs, as are angle, counter, constant and mostly-missing channels. Gaps up to one hour are forward-filled, the rest take the training median, and channels are standardised on training rows and clipped to $\pm10$.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Detectors}
The main detector is a dense autoencoder (AE), $d\!\to\!256\!\to\!64\!\to\!16\!\to\!64\!\to\!256\!\to\!d$ with ReLU and a linear output, trained with Adam ($10^{-3}$), batch 256, at most 40 epochs and early stopping after five epochs without improvement. Its score is the mean squared reconstruction error $e(t)=\tfrac{1}{d}\lVert \mathbf{x}(t)-\hat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\rVert_2^2$. Three variants test specific choices: the same AE with a sigmoid output, which cannot reproduce negative standardised values; a conditional AE (CAE) that receives the four scaled operating conditions at its input and next to its code; and an LSTM AE that encodes the last six rows (1~h) and scores row $t$ by the error of the window ending at $t$, so it never looks ahead. Baselines are Isolation Forest (IF, 200 trees) \cite{r16}, PCA keeping 95\% of variance, and the fraction of channels beyond 3$\sigma$. AE, CAE, LSTM AE and IF use seeds 0, 1 and 2.

\subsection{Condition normalisation and smoothing}
A healthy turbine's reconstruction error is not constant: at rated power, gusts, cut-in and cut-out the sensors move into regions that are rare in training, and the error rises although nothing is wrong. Let $\mathbf{u}(t)$ hold wind speed, active power, rotor speed and ambient temperature. A histogram gradient-boosting regressor $g$ (200 trees, rate 0.05) is fitted on $\mathcal{C}_a$, whose rows no detector was trained on (they serve only for early stopping), to predict the typical healthy error at each operating point:
\begin{equation}
r(t)=\log e(t)-g\big(\mathbf{u}(t)\big).
\end{equation}
$r(t)$ is the part of the error that the operating point does not explain; the logarithm compares observed and expected error as a ratio. Because the same error is now judged against an operating-point-specific expectation, the method acts like an adaptive threshold \cite{r12}. A causal 6-hour rolling median then removes isolated spikes, using only the current and past rows. Every detector is reported raw, smoothed, normalised, and with both steps.

\subsection{Thresholds, alarms and evaluation}
A row is flagged when its score exceeds $\tau$, the 99th percentile of the same score on $\mathcal{C}_b$. Alarms use the benchmark's criticality counter \cite{r12}:
\begin{equation}
c(t)=\begin{cases} c(t-1)+1 & \text{flagged, normal status}\\ \max\big(c(t-1)-1,0\big) & \text{not flagged, normal status}\\ c(t-1) & \text{abnormal status,}\end{cases}
\end{equation}
and an alarm is raised when $c(t)\ge72$, i.e.\ after 12~h of net abnormal behaviour.

The CARE score \cite{r12} has four parts. Coverage is the row-wise $F_{0.5}$ inside fault windows (how much of each fault is flagged); accuracy is the share of rows left unflagged in normal datasets (how quiet the detector is when nothing is wrong); reliability is the event-wise $F_{0.5}$ over all 95 datasets, counting a dataset as alarmed if $c$ ever reaches 72; and earliness weights flagged rows by their position in the event window, with weight one at the start falling linearly to zero at the end \cite{r12,r27}. The benchmark paper starts the fall halfway; we follow the authors' reference implementation, which starts it after the first quarter.\footnote{\url{https://aefdi.github.io/EnergyFaultDetector}, parameter \texttt{ws\_start\_of\_descend} = (1, 4).} They are combined as
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CARE}=\tfrac{1}{5}\big(\mathrm{Cov}+\mathrm{Earl}+\mathrm{Rel}+2\,\mathrm{Acc}\big),
\end{equation}
where accuracy counts twice so that the 50 normal periods weigh as much as the 45 faults. The score is 0 if nothing is detected and equals the accuracy if that falls to 0.5 or below; $F_{0.5}$ favours precision, so false alarms cost more than misses. Two configurations are compared with a paired bootstrap over datasets: fault and normal datasets are resampled separately 1000 times, both CARE scores are recomputed on each draw, and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of their difference give a 95\% interval.

The benchmark's 0.66 came from per-farm tuning \cite{r12}. To see how much tuning is worth without touching the test data, the quantile $q\in\{0.95,0.975,0.99,0.995,0.999\}$ and counter $k\in\{36,72,144\}$ are chosen by the seed-averaged CARE score on two farms and then applied unchanged to the third; repeating this for each farm gives a leave-one-farm-out (LOFO) score for all 95 datasets. Algorithm~\ref{alg:pipeline} summarises the scoring of one dataset.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Leakage-safe scoring of one turbine dataset}
\label{alg:pipeline}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require SCADA rows $\mathbf{x}(t)$, operating conditions $\mathbf{u}(t)$, status $s(t)$, train/test flag
\State $\mathcal{T}\gets$ training rows with $s\in\{0,2\}$, minus idle rows in good wind
\State Split $\mathcal{T}$ in time: fit rows $\mathcal{F}$ (85\%), then $\mathcal{C}_a$, $\mathcal{C}_b$ (7.5\% each)
\State Fit scaler, imputer and channel filter on $\mathcal{F}$ only; transform all rows
\State Train detector $D$ on $\mathcal{F}$, early-stop on $\mathcal{C}_a\cup\mathcal{C}_b$; $e(t)\gets D(\mathbf{x}(t))$
\State Fit $g$ on $\mathcal{C}_a$: $\log e \sim \mathbf{u}$; \ $r(t)\gets\log e(t)-g(\mathbf{u}(t))$
\State $\tilde r(t)\gets$ median of $r$ over the previous 6~h \Comment{causal}
\State $\tau\gets$ 99th percentile of $\tilde r$ on $\mathcal{C}_b$
\For{each prediction row $t$ in time order}
\State $c\gets c+1$ if $\tilde r(t)>\tau$ and $s(t)$ normal; $c\gets\max(c-1,0)$ if $\tilde r(t)\le\tau$ and $s(t)$ normal
\State \textbf{if} $c\ge 72$ \textbf{then} raise alarm; rank plant areas by channel error ratio
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Attribution, health index and robustness}
An alarm is more useful, and more trusted, when it says where to look \cite{r3}. Each channel is assigned to one of ten plant areas (pitch and hub, yaw, hydraulics and brakes, cooling, gearbox, generator, rotor and drive train, nacelle and tower, electrical, environment) by ordered keyword rules on its published description. For channel $j$ the error ratio
\begin{equation}
\rho_j=\frac{\overline{\varepsilon_j}\ \text{over the fault window}}{\overline{\varepsilon_j}\ \text{over held-out healthy rows}}
\end{equation}
measures how much worse the AE reconstructs it during the fault than when healthy, with $\varepsilon_j$ the squared error of channel $j$; dividing by the healthy level stops channels that are always hard to reconstruct from dominating. Areas are ranked by the mean ratio of their channels, by the summed ratio (which favours large areas), or by how many of the ten highest-ratio channels they own. The authors mapped each operator note to one or more areas; five vague notes were left out, leaving 40 faults. Floors are a random ranking, always naming the commonest area (an oracle, since it sees the labels), and the mean-ratio rule on absolute z-scores, which needs no model.

For maintenance planning the normalised, smoothed score is mapped to a health index
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{HI}(t)=\Big(1+\frac{\max(0,\tilde r(t)-q_{50})}{q_{99}-q_{50}}\Big)^{-1},
\end{equation}
with $q_{50}$ and $q_{99}$ the median and 99th percentile of $\tilde r$ on $\mathcal{C}_b$: HI is 1 for typical healthy behaviour, 0.5 exactly at the alarm threshold and falls towards 0 beyond it. A least-squares line through the last 24~h of HI gives a slope $s$; if $s<0$, the time to a floor of 0.2 is $(\mathrm{HI}-0.2)/(-s)$, capped at 720~h, and $s\pm1.96\,\mathrm{SE}(s)$ gives a 95\% band. A work order is suggested when an alarm is active and this time falls below a gate $G\in\{72,168,336\}$~h. With no replacement dates in the data, $G$ cannot be fitted, so all three are reported. To probe sensor faults, every prediction window is corrupted with Gaussian noise ($\sigma=0.1$--$0.5$) or 5--20\% of entries set to the training median, and rescored without retraining or moving the threshold; operating conditions are left clean.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Detection on 95 datasets}
Table~\ref{tab:care} gives the CARE score of each detector. With the fixed settings the plain AE reaches 0.584 and the full pipeline 0.592, clearly above random but below the tuned benchmark AE. Condition normalisation alone lowers the score to 0.568: it raises accuracy on normal periods from 0.962 to 0.970 and cuts alarms there from 5 to 4, but detected faults fall from 11.3 to 10.3. A likely reason is that part of the fault signal rises with load, so the regressor learns to expect it \cite{r26}. The paired bootstrap finds none of the AE post-processing differences reliable (smoothing over raw $+0.011$, 95\% CI $-0.015$ to $0.044$; normalisation over raw $-0.017$, $-0.039$ to $0.008$), and PCA is level with the AE ($+0.007$, $-0.030$ to $0.044$). The CAE matches the AE with slightly fewer false alarms ($+0.006$, $-0.001$ to $0.015$). The LSTM AE is significantly lower ($-0.054$, $-0.092$ to $-0.019$), and the sigmoid output that an earlier version of this pipeline used costs $0.086$ ($0.025$ to $0.146$): its median best validation loss is 0.71 against 0.20 for the linear head. Post-processing matters most for weak detectors: IF rises from 0.471 to 0.518 ($+0.047$, $0.003$ to $0.091$). The full AE pipeline detects 15.3 of 45 faults (rate 0.34, 95\% CI 0.21--0.48) with alarms in 7 of 50 normal periods, a median first alarm 147~h before the end of the fault window, and works best on farm A (5 of 12 faults, no false alarm) and worst on farm C (8 of 27), whose pitch, hub-electronics and communication faults leave little trace in 10-minute averages. At the event level (Table~\ref{tab:farm}) the neural detectors and PCA reach a precision of 0.64--0.69 at a recall of 0.23--0.36: when they alarm they are usually right, but most faults pass unflagged. No detector is best on every farm: PCA leads on farm C, the largest, the AEs lead on farms A and B, and the LSTM AE raises no alarm at all on farm B, which sets its CARE score there to zero by definition.

\subsection{What tuning is worth}
Fig.~\ref{fig:care} compares the fixed setting with LOFO tuning. Tuning chose the lowest quantile (0.95, sometimes 0.975) for almost every detector and farm, so the fixed 99th percentile was too strict. It lifts the full AE pipeline from 0.592 to 0.602, the LSTM AE from 0.564 to 0.607, PCA from 0.598 to 0.611 and IF from 0.518 to 0.582; the best tuned score is 0.615 (LSTM AE with smoothing). Even tuning on the test data itself, an optimistic ceiling, gives at most 0.619 on our grid. Threshold and counter choice therefore close only part of the gap to 0.66; the rest presumably lies in the per-farm architecture and learning-rate tuning of \cite{r12}, which we did not repeat. A shorter counter (36) raises detections and false alarms together and leaves the CARE score almost unchanged, while 144 lowers it for every detector.

\subsection{Attribution against operator notes}
Table~\ref{tab:attribution} scores the ranking rules. The vote rule names a correct area first in 37\% of faults and within the top three in 64\%, well above a random ranking (12\%, 35\%) and above the model-free z-score rule (22\%, 48\%), so the AE adds information beyond raw deviations. It only matches, however, always naming pitch and hub, the commonest area (30\%, 70\%). On farm C the vote rule (48\% top-1) falls short of always answering pitch (55\%), and on farm A no rule beats chance at top-1 (8\%) while always answering hydraulics gives 50\%. Attribution narrows the search but is not yet better than an operator's prior on what usually fails.

\subsection{Case studies}
Dataset 40 (farm A) ends in a generator-bearing failure. The full pipeline raised its first alarm about 45~h after the start of the 31-day fault window and kept alarming through it (Fig.~\ref{fig:hi}); generator was the top-ranked area (mean error ratio 13.1, ahead of gearbox at 12.9). At every gate the first work order fell on that first alarm, about 29 days before the window ended, with no trigger in $\mathcal{C}_b$ or before the event (Fig.~\ref{fig:ttt}). The score was also high on rows just before the window and on two short episodes in the training year. Dataset 44 (farm C, a cooling-water valve left in the wrong position after maintenance) was missed: only 20 of 8788 window rows crossed the threshold, although the cooling area still ranked first of ten. A gradient-boosting model of cooling-water outlet temperature (MAE 1.4~$^\circ$C, $R^2=0.31$ on later rows) shows a residual shift of only 0.6~$^\circ$C during the fault, well inside its error, consistent with the miss.

\subsection{Robustness and compute cost}
Corrupting the prediction window without moving the threshold mainly raises false alarms (Fig.~\ref{fig:robust}): for the full AE pipeline (seed 0), alarms in normal periods grow from 6 to 13, 25 and 30 of 50 at noise $\sigma=0.1$, 0.3 and 0.5, and to 19 and 27 with 5\% and 20\% missing entries, while accuracy falls from 0.95 to 0.52. The CARE score barely moves (0.546--0.611) because extra detections in fault windows offset the false alarms, which shows that the score alone can hide a sensor problem. The threshold must therefore be recalibrated when sensor quality changes. On a Kaggle T4 GPU the median fit time per farm-C turbine-year was 8.0~s for the AE, 21.9~s for the LSTM AE, 2.8~s for PCA and 0.7~s for IF, and scoring 1000 rows took at most 10~ms, so every detector is cheap enough for a 10-minute SCADA stream on a single edge computer. \section{Limitations}
\textit{What the data cannot show.} Ten-minute averages smooth away short electrical and communication faults, so no detector change recovers them. \textit{Life figure.} The time to threshold is a short-horizon slope to a chosen floor; the benchmark publishes no replacement times, so no remaining-useful-life error can be computed. \textit{Labels.} Plant areas were assigned to brief operator notes by the authors, and several faults involve more than one area. \textit{Scope.} One architecture per family was tested with fixed hyperparameters; per-farm architecture tuning is left open.

\section{Conclusion}
On all 95 CARE-to-Compare datasets, with time-ordered splits and settings fixed in advance, a dense autoencoder reaches a CARE score of 0.584. Condition normalisation and smoothing move it by less than the variation between datasets, PCA and a conditional autoencoder do as well, an LSTM autoencoder does worse, and a sigmoid output does markedly worse. Normalisation lowers false alarms but hides load-dependent fault signal, and pays off for weak detectors such as Isolation Forest. Thresholds tuned leave-one-farm-out raise each detector's full pipeline to 0.58--0.61, which explains only part of the gap to a per-farm tuned result. Attribution beats a random ranking and a model-free ranking but only matches the commonest-area prior. Next steps are per-farm architecture search under the same leakage rules, fitting the work-order gate to a maintenance log, and transferring the condition model between turbines \cite{r18}.
